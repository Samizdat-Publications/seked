Keep going. The fidelity is there.

You asked whether this could ever look the way you want. It can. What held it back was not effort and not the data. It was where the detail lived and what was drawing it. The first station of the walkthrough was built in an afternoon from numbers the project already had, laid block by block, and lit by a path tracer instead of a game renderer.

Since the first station

The same evening, the walkthrough grew into this:

  • Five eras on one timeline. The First Time, c. 10,500 BCE, drawn as the lost-civilisation claim describes it: green savanna, pristine casing, the Anubis Sphinx. The lion era, c. 7000 BCE, drier, also a claim. The plateau as built, c. 2560 BCE, with gold and electrum caps, enclosure walls, dressed mastabas, the roofed causeway, the red granite temples, the harbour with its quay and boats, and the Wall of the Crow. The plateau of c. 1800, the Sphinx buried to its chest. And today.
  • Fourteen stations. Among them a papyrus raft on the harbour, the court of Khufu's mortuary temple, the Eastern Cemetery, Menkaure with his granite casing and three queens, a close view of the Sphinx's face in each of its forms, and three inside the Great Pyramid: the Grand Gallery, the King's Chamber with the coffer where Petrie found it, and the Queen's Chamber with its corbelled niche, all from his interior survey.
  • People. Men on the quay and priests in the temple court as built, and visitors today: figures generated for the project and labelled as look choices, not portraits of anyone.

Drag across each frame. The present day is laid from blocks: Khufu's 201 courses, Khafre's surviving cap with its ragged lower edge, Menkaure's band of red granite casing. What was holding it back

scale

Detail at the survey's scale, none at the eye's

The old scene modelled a pyramid as five faces and a temple as its footprint pushed up into a box. The eye reads Giza at the scale of blocks: 201 courses on Khufu, a ragged skyline where the corners were robbed, rubble at every foot, sand in every step. None of that existed, so no amount of lighting could make it look real.

light

A game renderer

The web viewer drew everything in real time, which means no true bounce light. At Giza the bright sand lights the pyramids from below, and without it every face reads flat. Much of the last week went into frame rates rather than into the stone.

weight

Proof paperwork on every stone

Every visual element had to be a sourced record, a TypeScript builder, a Python mirror and a test that the two agree. That is right for a claim and far too heavy for a pebble.

What changes

Blender's path tracer draws it

Light is computed, not approximated: bounce light off the sand, the soft shadow of the whole sky, the sun reddened by the air it crosses.

Everything at the scale you see it

Pyramids course by course from measured course heights where they exist. Blocks sized, weathered and missing by rule, and labelled as reconstruction.

A walkthrough of stations

Each station is a 360° path-traced panorama like the ones in the walkthrough, in each era, with the slider between them. Flights between stations are pre-rendered film.

The claims draw on top

Every station's camera is known exactly, so the claims engine and the star calculations can draw their lines and stars over the panorama live, registered to the pixel.

What stays: the measurement database and its sources, the sky engine with its precession and star catalogue, the claims engine and the Claude claims runner, the rule that a reconstruction is always labelled, and the current web viewer, kept as the survey tool rather than the showcase.
