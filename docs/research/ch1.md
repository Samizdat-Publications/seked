## 1. The claim at full strength

The Sphinx was not built. It was carved, by digging a ditch around a knoll of bedrock, so the ditch walls are exactly as old as the statue. A geologist looking at those walls without knowing what they are would say rain did this. The profile rolls: harder beds stand out and softer beds are cut back into deep coves. Vertical fissures open widest at the top, where water running off the plateau would enter. Wind and sand cut horizontal grooves, and Old Kingdom tombs cut into the same plateau show that kind of wear, not this kind.

Under the ditch floor, seismic refraction finds the rock weathered 1.8 to 2.5 m deep in front of the Sphinx and along its sides, and as much as about 4 m at one line. Behind the rump, where the ditch looks like a later extension, it is about 1.2 m. If depth grows with exposure, the core of the statue was cut long before Khafre, and the rains that weathered it ended in the Neolithic.

On the most conservative assumption, a straight-line weathering rate, the minimum date is 7000 to 5000 BCE. Schoch now favours about 10,000 BCE, the end of the last Ice Age. Khafre inherited the Sphinx and restored it; he did not make it. The core seismic result was published in a peer-reviewed geology journal, and Egyptology has answered it with an inference about building sequence, not with a dated measurement of the weathering.

## 2. The proponents' evidence

**1. The ditch walls show the profile rain makes.**
- **The observation:** The ditch walls, and to a lesser degree the body, have a rolling, coved profile, with vertical fissures widest at the top. Erosion is heaviest at the west end of the ditch and fades eastward.
- **Who and where:** Schoch, R. M. (1992), "Redating the Great Sphinx of Giza", *KMT: A Modern Journal of Ancient Egypt* 3(2), Summer 1992. Page not found. **[unchecked]**: the volume and issue come from a Schoch-related page seen in search results. It was presented first with J. A. West at the Geological Society of America meeting in San Diego, 23 October 1991 **[unchecked: secondary accounts only; the abstract itself was not found]**. Schoch's current statement is at robertschoch.com/sphinx.html. It describes heavy precipitation weathering on the body and walls, heaviest at the west end, with fissures showing "precipitation and runoff" **[checked]**.
- **Peer review:** *KMT* is a popular magazine and not peer-reviewed; a GSA abstract is not peer-reviewed either.
- **How strong:** This is the heart of the case. It is wrong if the coves and fissures are mostly older than the carving, or if arid-climate processes can make them. Gauri's karst finding (reply 2) and the salt mechanisms (reply 3) argue exactly that.

**2. Tombs cut in the same plateau show wind wear, not rain wear.**
- **The observation:** Old Kingdom tombs and quarry faces nearby have the sharp, horizontally scoured faces typical of wind. Schoch's page contrasts the Sphinx with Old Kingdom structures showing only wind and sand erosion **[checked: robertschoch.com/sphinx.html]**.
- **How strong:** It holds only if the comparison is between the same beds and the same exposure. Lehner argues it is not (reply 4).

**3. Weathering under the ditch floor is deeper in front than behind.**
- **The observation:** Seismic refraction, refraction tomography and reflection surveys were run in the ditch, around the Sphinx and in the desert nearby. The abstract says weathering under the ditch floor "is not uniform", which "suggests" that different parts were exposed for different lengths of time.
- **Citation:** Dobecki, T. L. & Schoch, R. M. (1992). "Seismic investigations in the vicinity of the Great Sphinx of Giza, Egypt". *Geoarchaeology* 7(6), 527–544, doi:10.1002/gea.3340070603. **[checked: Crossref record and abstract]**. Existing source id: **`dobecki-schoch-1992`**. Peer-reviewed.
- **The figures:** I could not read the paper's own figures. Reader summarises them, apparently from the paper and from Schoch's *KMT* article:
  - Line S3, behind the rump: about 1.2 m.
  - Lines S1, S2 and S4, along the flanks and in front of the paws: 1.8 to 2.5 m in *KMT*.
  - The 1992 paper puts S4 at about 4 m.
  - Line S9, across the Sphinx Temple floor (left out of *KMT*): 1.2 to 1.5 m. That floor is cut about 3 m lower than the ditch floor.
  - Source for all four: Reader, C., *Giza Before the Fourth Dynasty*, 18 pp., Fig. 5 caption **[checked: the paper's academia.edu page; its venue and year are not given there]**.
- **How Schoch gets a date:** His page says the seismic data, "calibrated very conservatively", give a minimum age of at least 7,000 years for the core body, and about 12,000 years is "more realistic" **[checked: robertschoch.com/sphinx.html]**. The arithmetic is reproduced in §3.
- **How strong:** This is the only peer-reviewed and quantitative support for the old date. It fails if seismic velocity is tracking the original character of the rock rather than weathering, if the rear boundary was picked too high (reply 5), or if exposure differed for reasons other than age, such as sand burial or groundwater.

**4. The temples' core blocks are weathered too, under their granite.**
- **The observation:** The limestone core blocks of the Sphinx Temple and the Valley Temple show the same precipitation weathering beneath their Aswan granite facing. Schoch reads this as granite laid over blocks that were already weathered **[checked: robertschoch.com/sphinx.html]**. This belongs mainly to chapter 3.
- **How strong:** It fails if the limestone was dressed to fit the granite, which is Lehner's reading from Menkaure's unfinished temple (reply 4).

**5. The date has moved older, and has gained an archaeological context.**
- **The dates:**
  - The original 1991–92 minimum: before c. 5000 BCE, put as 7000 to 5000 BCE.
  - Schoch's page now: "the end of the last ice age, circa 10,000 BCE" **[checked: robertschoch.com/sphinx.html]**.
  - Schoch, R. M. (2012), *Forgotten Civilization: The Role of Solar Outbursts in Our Past and Future*, Inner Traditions. It ties the end of the Ice Age at 9700 BCE to solar outbursts **[checked: bookseller and publisher listings only]**. A second edition, with Catherine Ulissey, came out in 2021 **[checked: robertschoch.com home page]**.
  - Schoch, R. M. & Bauval, R. (2017), *Origins of the Sphinx: Celestial Guardian of Pre-Pharaonic Civilization*, Inner Traditions, 16 March 2017. The Sphinx was first built about 12,000 years ago, at the same time as Göbekli Tepe, and recarved in the Old Kingdom. The book includes a "reanalysis of seismic studies" and treats Zep Tepi as c. 10,500 to 9700 BCE **[checked: publisher's page]**.
- **The new argument:** Answering Egyptologists who asked where the earlier civilisation was, Schoch now points to Göbekli Tepe **[checked: robertschoch.com/sphinx.html]**.
- **Peer review:** None of these is peer-reviewed.
- **How strong:** Göbekli Tepe answers the "no culture capable of it" objection in principle; it is not evidence about Giza. The move from 7000 to 10,000 BCE is not driven by new Giza measurements that I could find, and that makes the later date weaker than the original minimum.

**6. Where the idea came from, and how Hancock and Bauval use it.**
- **The origin:** West, J. A. (1979), *Serpent in the Sky*, following Schwaller de Lubicz. West attributed the erosion to water and brought Schoch in **[unchecked; via the Wikipedia article "Sphinx water erosion hypothesis"]**. Then came the NBC documentary *The Mystery of the Sphinx* (1993) **[unchecked]**.
- **How Hancock and Bauval use it:** `hancock-1995` (*Fingerprints of the Gods*) and `hancock-bauval-1996` (*Keeper of Genesis*) take Schoch's geology as the independent physical support for an epoch around 10,500 BCE, which they get from Leo and Orion. Hancock, G. (2015), *Magicians of the Gods*, returns to Schoch alongside Göbekli Tepe. **[unchecked: page not found for any of the three]**.
- **How strong:** In the proponents' argument, the geology carries the weight; the astronomy then picks the epoch. If the geology fails, 10,500 BCE rests on the sky alone.

**7. Geologists who agree that the Sphinx is older than Khafre, but only a little older.**
- **Colin Reader:**
  - Reader, C. D. (2001). "A Geomorphological Study of the Giza Necropolis, with Implications for the Development of the Site". *Archaeometry* 43(1), 149–165, doi:10.1111/1475-4754.00009 **[checked: Crossref abstract]**. Peer-reviewed.
  - The erosion shows flowing water. Its uneven distribution points to rainfall run-off, which is known at Giza until the late Fifth Dynasty. Khufu's quarries upslope later cut the ditch off from that run-off, so the Sphinx probably predates Khufu.
  - In 2026 Reader went further: *Rewriting the History of the Great Sphinx* (Archaeopress, 2026, ISBN 978-1-805-83209-6). The Sphinx was carved in the Second Dynasty, its head was first the lion goddess Mafdet and was recarved under Khufu, and the Sphinx Temple was built in phases with Khafre's Valley Temple later **[checked: review in *The Past*, 16 April 2026, which judges that the book "fundamentally alters our understanding of the archaeology of Giza"; the book itself not read]**.
- **David Coxill:** a geologist who agreed the weathering needs rain and a pre-dynastic date. Coxill, D. (1998), "The Riddle of the Sphinx", *Inscription: Journal of Ancient Egypt* 2. **[unchecked: I could not find this article online; the details are from memory]**.
- **How strong:** Reader is the strongest ally on mechanism, because he accepts water erosion in a peer-reviewed paper. He is also the strongest enemy of the date, because his water is Old Kingdom run-off and his Sphinx is c. 2700 to 2900 BCE.

## 4. The archaeologists' reply

**1. The building sequence puts the Sphinx late in Khafre's reign.**
- **The argument, as a chain of physical relationships:**
  - Khafre's Valley Temple enclosure wall is built around the temple's granite casing.
  - Part of that wall's north side was reused as the Sphinx Temple's south wall, so the Sphinx Temple is later than the Valley Temple.
  - The Sphinx Temple's core blocks match the lower Member II beds of the Sphinx quarry, bed for bed, which puts the temple and the Sphinx's lower body in one quarry-and-build sequence.
  - The quarry's south edge forms the north side of the causeway's foundation.
  - AERA's conclusion: the Sphinx and its temple date "with a high degree of probability" to "late in the sequence" of Khafre's building programme.
- **Citations:** AERA, "Why sequence is important", aeraweb.org, undated **[checked]**. Hawass, Z. & Lehner, M. (1994), "The Sphinx: Who built it, and why?", *Archaeology* 47(5), 30–41 **[unchecked: the pages are from JSTOR's issue listing seen in search; the text was not read]**. Lehner, M. (1992), "Reconstructing the Sphinx", *Cambridge Archaeological Journal* 2(1), 3–26 **[unchecked: the fetch failed]**. Also `arce-sphinx-1991`, `lehner-hawass-2017`.
- **Peer review:** CAJ is peer-reviewed; *Archaeology* and AERA's site are not.
- **How strong:** Very strong for when the ditch was quarried as a source of building stone. It is weaker against the steelman's fallback: a smaller, older core enlarged by Khafre's quarrymen. The sequence dates the last cutting, not necessarily the first. *(Strongest reply.)*

**2. The fissures are old karst, opened up by the digging, not cut by later rain.**
- **The argument:** Weathering in an arid climate can produce the rounded profile, given how gradually the hard and soft beds grade into each other. The channels in the ditch walls are "pre-Pliocene karst features" that the digging exposed.
- **Citation:** Gauri, K. L., Sinai, J. J. & Bandyopadhyay, J. K. (1995). "Geologic weathering and its implications on the age of the Sphinx". *Geoarchaeology* 10(2), 119–133, doi:10.1002/gea.3340100203 **[checked: Crossref record and abstract]**. Peer-reviewed, in the same journal as Dobecki & Schoch.
- **Support:** Christiansen, J. (2015), "The Great Sphinx of Egypt: Nature's Shabby-Chic Trick?", *GEO ExPro* web exclusive, 1 December 2015. Acid water weakened the fractured limestone long before the carving, so its mark cannot be separated from later weathering: "From a geological point of view I did not find any evidence that could date the carving of the Sphinx" **[checked]**. Not peer-reviewed. Christiansen also finds that the ditch wall is aligned with Khafre's causeway and pyramid.
- **How strong:** Strong against the fissures as a clock. It does not by itself explain the floor seismic.

**3. Salts and dew wear soft limestone fast, with no rain needed.**
- **Gauri:** Salt crystallisation (haloclasty) driven by moisture. Gauri, K. L. (1981), "Deterioration of the stone of the Great Sphinx", *ARCE Newsletter* 114, 35–47 **[unchecked: a reproduction seen in search]**. Gauri later moved the moisture source from groundwater to dew, which dissolves salts that then crystallise as halite and gypsum when it dries **[unchecked; secondary]**. The Getty Conservation Institute's 1990–92 study found salt crystallisation behind at least some of the decay **[unchecked; via Wikipedia]**.
- **Harrell, wet sand:** the deep weathering is "a post-Old Kingdom development resulting from the periodic wetting of the limestone by a water-saturated sand cover". Sand filled the ditch for most of its history and was soaked by Nile floods and rain. Flood water rose 1 to 2 m by capillary action, clays swelled, and salts precipitated in the marly beds. Harrell, J. A. (1994), "The Sphinx Controversy: Another Look at the Geological Evidence", *KMT* 5(2), 70–74 **[checked: Hall of Maat reprint]**. Not peer-reviewed.
- **How strong:** Moderately strong. It shows rain is not the only explanation, but none of these authors has dated the weathering either.

**4. The comparisons are between different rock layers.**
- **The argument:**
  - The Debehen tomb and the escarpment tombs are in different strata from the Sphinx's Member II, about 420 m away and 17 to 43 m higher: "he is comparing different rock layers".
  - The west wall's vertical cracks are natural joints.
  - West and Schoch have it "both ways": the west wall is their best rain evidence, yet they also say Khafre cut that end back.
  - At Menkaure's unfinished temple, the masons are seen shaping limestone to fit granite, which answers the "granite over weathered blocks" point (evidence 4).
  - The Saqqara mud-brick tombs were buried by the Third Dynasty, so they are no test.
  - Two temples exposed about 85 to 90 years apart show different modern decay rates by location.
- **Citation:** Lehner, M. (1994), "Notes and Photographs on the West-Schoch Sphinx Hypothesis", *KMT* 5(3), 40–48 **[checked: Hall of Maat reprint]**. Not peer-reviewed. The article does not address the seismic data.
- **On the rock:** Member I is 3.70 m thick at the rump and 0.65 to 1.09 m at the front paws (same reprint) **[checked]**. The floor itself changes in rock type front to back, which bears directly on the seismic comparison.
- **How strong:** Strong on the tomb comparison; it leaves the floor seismic unanswered.

**5. The seismic difference may be the rock, not the age.**
- **Harrell (1994, as above) [checked]:**
  - The low-velocity layers could reflect the rock as it was laid down (coral-rich "shoal reef" limestone), not weathering.
  - The rear line S3's velocities suggest its weathered/sound boundary was picked too high; lowered, all three profiles look alike.
- **Reader's figures [checked: academia.edu page]:** the Sphinx Temple floor (S9) weathers 1.2 to 1.5 m. Like the rear, that is shallower than the front, so the "deep" zone is local to the ditch and not a plateau-wide age signal.
- **How strong:** Moderate. No one has re-shot the lines or drilled to calibrate them, so neither reading of the seismic has been tested.

**6. There was rain at Giza in Khufu's and Khafre's own time.**
- **The climate record:**
  - The Eastern Sahara was humid from c. 8500 BCE and dried progressively from 5300 BCE: Kuper, R. & Kröpelin, S. (2006), "Climate-Controlled Holocene Occupation in the Sahara: Motor of Africa's Evolution", *Science* 313, 803–807, doi:10.1126/science.1130989 **[checked: the abstract's content as seen in search; the paper not read]**.
  - The end of the African Humid Period was locally abrupt but later at lower latitudes: Shanahan, T. M. et al. (2015), "The time-transgressive termination of the African humid period", *Nature Geoscience* 8, 140–144 **[checked: listing and abstract seen in search]**.
  - The classic abrupt end c. 5,500 years ago: deMenocal, P. et al. (2000), "Abrupt onset and termination of the African Humid Period", *Quaternary Science Reviews* 19, 347–361 **[unchecked; from memory]**.
  - Episodic heavy rain at Saqqara until about 4200 BP, c. 2200 BCE: Welc, F. & Marks, L. (2014), "Climate change at the end of the Old Kingdom in Egypt around 4200 BP: New geoarchaeological evidence", *Quaternary International* 324, 124–133, doi:10.1016/j.quaint.2013.07.035 **[metadata checked via Crossref; the finding unchecked, via Wikipedia]**.
  - Reader's argument (reply 1's neighbour, evidence 7) depends on that late rain.
- **How strong:** Strong against "the rains ended before the pharaohs". Rain-induced weathering is possible after Khafre. The open question is whether rain over a few centuries is enough.

**7. The only physical dates on the Sphinx complex are dynastic.**
- **The data:** Surface luminescence dating, Table 1, p. 137:
  - Sphinx Temple limestone, at the base of the outer wall in contact with bedrock in front of the Sphinx (sample RHO-56/ST2): 2220 ± 220 BCE.
  - Sphinx Temple granites: ST3 1190 ± 340 BCE; ST4 2740 ± 640 BCE; ST5 3100 ± 540 BCE.
  - Valley Temple: granite VT1 3060 ± 470 BCE; limestone VT9a 1050 ± 540 BCE.
  - The abstract says the results "confirmed most conventional Dynastic dates", some earlier by "some hundred of years".
- **Citation:** Liritzis, I. & Vafiadou, A. (2015), "Surface luminescence dating of some Egyptian monuments", *Journal of Cultural Heritage* 16, 134–150, doi:10.1016/j.culher.2014.05.007 **[checked: PDF, Table 1 p. 137]**. Peer-reviewed.
- **How strong:** Weak to moderate. The method is young, there are few samples and the errors are wide. It dates when the blocks were set, not when the statue was cut. Still, nothing reaches the fourth millennium BCE, let alone the tenth.

**8. The yardang experiments of 2023–24: the shape may be partly natural.**
- **The work:**
  - Boury, S., Weady, S. & Ristroph, L. (2023), "Sculpting the Sphinx", *Physical Review Fluids* 8, 110503, doi:10.1103/PhysRevFluids.8.110503, CC BY 4.0 **[checked: PDF]**. A Gallery of Fluid Motion paper. Clay mounds with a hard inclusion, eroded by flowing water, become a "lion in repose", with the hard core as the head.
  - Boury, S., Weady, S. & Ristroph, L. (2024), "Yardangs sculpted by erosion of heterogeneous material", *PNAS* 121(30), e2322411121, doi:10.1073/pnas.2322411121 **[checked: Crossref record]**. Peer-reviewed; the Sphinx appears only in its references.
  - These revive Farouk El-Baz's 1981 idea of a natural yardang knoll **[unchecked]**.
- **How strong:** Not a dating study at all. It bears on chapter 2, the "head too small" argument, and on how much carving the knoll needed, not on how old the weathering is. It should not be presented as a reply on age.

**9. Newer geological work on the ditch: not found.**
- I found no 2020s peer-reviewed study of the ditch walls' weathering or age, despite two searches, one of them extended. The newest substantive geology is still Reader (2001, and his 2026 book, which is archaeology) and Christiansen (2015, not peer-reviewed).
- Vandecruys, G. (2006), a reply to Reader in *PalArch's Journal of Archaeology of Egypt/Egyptology* **[unchecked: only a listing found; the title is uncertain]**.

## 5. Where it stands

Both sides now accept that water shaped the ditch. The dispute is over when, and how much water. The two strongest pieces of evidence are not about the same thing. Schoch's peer-reviewed seismic shows the floor weathered unevenly; the building sequence ties the ditch's quarrying to Khafre's temples. Neither has been tested by the obvious experiment: drilling or re-surveying the floor to calibrate weathering depth against a dated surface.

Among geologists who have published on it, the case for an older Sphinx has shrunk to Reader's few centuries, now argued as a Second Dynasty Sphinx in a 2026 book. Schoch's 10,000 BCE rests on a straight-line extrapolation he himself calls conservative, plus a cultural argument from Göbekli Tepe, not on new Giza data.

## 7. Open questions

- **The seismic depths themselves.** The paper's own figures, sigmas and the S4 value of about 4 m were read only through Reader. Its tables and figures need reading before anything goes into `data/`. So does the *KMT* 1992 page range.
- **Is the floor the same rock front and back?** Member I thins from 3.70 m at the rump to under 1.1 m at the paws (Lehner 1994). Whether the seismic contrast follows rock type rather than exposure is the crux. No published re-analysis was found, and the "reanalysis" in *Origins of the Sphinx* (2017) was not read.
- **Exposure history.** How long the ditch was buried in sand, front and back, is unknown. The weathering clock assumes equal, continuous exposure.
- **How fast rain-weathering works.** No rate for this limestone has been measured that could turn Old Kingdom rain into a depth. Without one, neither "few centuries" (Reader, Welc & Marks) nor "millennia" (Schoch) is quantitative.
- **The Schoch–Lehner tension at the west end.** Schoch's best rain evidence (the west wall) and his shallow, late floor (S3) are at the same end of the ditch. I did not find his reply to Lehner's "both ways" point.
- **Not confirmed:**
  - The 1991 GSA abstract.
  - Coxill 1998.
  - Vandecruys 2006.
  - Hawass & Lehner 1994's text.
  - Lehner 1992 in CAJ.
  - Gauri 1981.
  - West 1979's page.
  - Hancock's chapters in the 1995, 1996 and 2015 books.
  - Welc & Marks's finding.
  - deMenocal 2000.
  - Hawass, *The Secrets of the Sphinx* (1998).
  - Lehner's argument that the earliest repair masonry, about 1,100 years after Khafre, already sits on deeply weathered Member II (a strong reply on rate if confirmed; seen only via Wikipedia).
