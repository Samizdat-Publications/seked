# Chapter 2 research brief: A lion, not a king

## 1. The claim at full strength

The Sphinx is a lion carved out of the living rock, and its head does not fit its body. Next to the 73 m body the human head is plainly too small. The head is in better condition than the body, and it shows the nemes and the face of a Fourth Dynasty king. The simplest reading is that the head was re-cut. The figure was first a lion, or some other animal, and it had weathered for a long time before a pharaoh, most likely Khafre, had a royal face carved into what was left of its head. That would explain why the head is undersized: you can only carve a smaller form inside a larger one.

The face itself does not clearly belong to Khafre. A forensic artist commissioned for John Anthony West in 1993 found that it did not match Khafre's statue. Even Egyptologists disagree about which king it shows.

Then ask why a lion, and why facing due east. On the morning of the vernal equinox the sun rises due east of the Sphinx in every era. But only in the eleventh millennium BCE did the constellation of the Lion rise on that horizon just before the sun, so that the sun came up "in" Leo. If the monument was meant to watch its own sky image at the turn of the year, the sky gives a date of about 10,500 BCE. That date fits the weathering of the Sphinx (chapter 1) and the Orion-belt date for the plateau's plan (chapter 5). Hancock and Bauval do not claim this proves the Sphinx is that old. They claim the lion, the due-east gaze and the sky agree with each other, and that nothing in the Fourth Dynasty explains them as well. (≈245 words)

## 2. The proponents' evidence

1. **The head is too small for the body.**
   - *Who says it:* West, Schoch, Hancock and Bauval, Temple, and now Reader.
   - *Citations:*
     - West, *Serpent in the Sky*, 2nd ed., 1993, pp. 230–232 on the face. **[unchecked]**: the pages come from catchpenny.org (fetched), which cites them.
     - Schoch & Bauval, *Origins of the Sphinx*, Inner Traditions, 2017. The publisher's description says the Sphinx "was recarved during the Old Kingdom". **[checked: publisher description only; the pages for the head argument were not found]**
     - Reader 2026, pp. 245–250, an original lion later re-cut as a king. **[checked: through Lacovara's review, which quotes the pages]**
   - *Peer review:* none of these is peer reviewed. Reader is published by Archaeopress, an academic press; whether the book was refereed is not stated.
   - *Strength:* this is the best visual point in the chapter, and nobody disputes the observation itself. To be wrong, the size of the head would have to have another cause. Egyptologists offer one (reply items 2 and 3).
   - *Gap:* nobody has published a quantitative head-to-body comparison against a lion or against other Egyptian sphinxes. I found no such measurement. Seked could make one (§3).

2. **The original was a lion, re-cut.**
   - *Variants:*
     - Schoch: the original head was weathered and then re-carved smaller in dynastic times, possibly from a lion's head. **[unchecked: secondary summaries only]**
     - Reader 2026: a Second Dynasty lion, perhaps the lion goddess Mafdet (pp. 190–213, 215–232, 245–250). **[checked through Lacovara]**
   - *Strength:* this follows from item 1 but goes further than it. A re-cut does not by itself make the original prehistoric. Reader's own version has the lion only a few centuries older than Khafre.

3. **Robert Temple's Anubis variant.**
   - *Claim:* the Sphinx was first a huge recumbent jackal, Anubis, sitting in a water-filled moat (the Pyramid Texts' "Jackal Lake"). Its face is a Middle Kingdom re-carving of Amenemhet II.
   - *Citation:* Temple, R. with Temple, O., *The Sphinx Mystery: The Forgotten Origins of the Sanctuary of Anubis*, Inner Traditions, 2009. **[checked: publisher and retailer descriptions as returned by search; the book's pages were not found]**
   - *Peer review:* none.
   - *Strength:* weak. It accepts that the head was re-cut but proposes a different original animal and a different king. For a steelman it shows that "re-cut head" does not lead only to "lion". Its dating of the face is Middle Kingdom, not prehistoric.

4. **The face is not Khafre's: the Domingo comparison.**
   - *What was done:* Frank Domingo, a senior forensic artist with the NYPD, made scaled drawings of the Sphinx's face and of the diorite Khafre in Cairo (CG 14) for West, in 1992–93. He concluded they show different people. He pointed to the angle of the jaw and the prognathism of the Sphinx's face. The work was broadcast in NBC's *Mystery of the Sphinx* (1993, presented by Charlton Heston), and Hancock and Bauval repeat it in *Keeper of Genesis*.
   - *Citations:* **[unchecked]**. The details come from secondary web summaries. I could not find a published report by Domingo himself, and the broadcast year is given as 1992, 1993 or 1996 depending on the source.
   - *Related source:* Galassi, F. M. (2014), "On the face and identity of the Great Sphinx of Giza: a medico-anthropological review", *Shemu* (Egyptian Society of South Africa). He accepts the mandibular prognathism and calls the Khafre attribution "circumstantial". **[checked: title, author and journal through the Academia.edu listing; pages not found. Shemu is a society journal, not a strictly peer-reviewed one]**
   - *Strength:* moderate as a talking point, weak as evidence. For it to hold, the Sphinx's face would have to be a portrait, carved in bedrock with portrait accuracy, and surviving without restoration. Note also that a face that is not Khafre's still leaves the head dynastic. Reader and Galassi propose other Fourth Dynasty kings, not a prehistoric face.

5. **Lehner's "reconstruction" was a superimposition.**
   - *Claim:* Hancock and Bauval argue that Lehner's computer model of the Sphinx with Khafre's face is circular. In their words he built a "wireframe skeleton on which he then superimposed the face of Khafre".
   - *Citation:* Hancock & Bauval 1996 (source id `hancock-bauval-1996`). **[unchecked: the quotation comes from a search summary of the book; page not found]**
   - *Strength:* fair on method. A model that adds Khafre's face cannot test whether the face is Khafre's. Lehner's case for Khafre rests on other grounds (reply item 1).

6. **The lion faces the equinox sunrise when Leo rose with the sun, c. 10,500 BCE.**
   - *Who says it:* Hancock & Bauval 1996 (`hancock-bauval-1996`); Hancock 1995 (`hancock-1995`); repeated by Hancock in *Magicians of the Gods* (2015) and by Schoch & Bauval (2017).
   - *Citations:*
     - The publisher's copy for Hancock & Bauval places the Sphinx at "the start of the astrological Age of Leo around 10,500 BC". **[checked: Apple Books description as returned by search; pages not found]**
     - Hancock's equal-sign "Age of Leo" of 10,970–8,810 BC. **[unchecked, from memory; page not found]**
   - *Peer review:* none.
   - *Strength:* the agreement is real but loose (see §3: Seked's C5 gives a mixed result). For it to be wrong you only need the Egyptians of any period not to have seen Leo as a lion (reply item 4). Seked can compute the rest.
   - *Background worth stating plainly:* the idea is older than Hancock. William Richard Hamilton's *Aegyptiaca* (1809) already considered and rejected a vernal-equinox Leo date of about 10,500 BCE. The astrologer Charles Hatfield (1901, p. 22) dated the Sphinx to about 11,044 BCE by the same method. **[checked: Colavito 2017, a blog post, fetched, citing both]**

7. **A newer, moderate proponent: Reader 2026.**
   - *Citation:* Reader, C. D., *Rewriting the History of the Great Sphinx*, Archaeopress Archaeology, 2026, 305 pp.
   - *Content:* a geologist's case for an Early Dynastic, pre-Khafre lion. It includes appendices on original ground levels, on the order in which the Sphinx, the valley temple and the Sphinx temple were built, and a map of the Member II strata. **[checked through the review]**
   - *Note:* it concedes that weathering cannot give an absolute date (p. 23). It is the most recent book-length proponent work, and the closest to mainstream.

## 3. Receipts

**Already computed:**

- **C5 "Sphinx and Leo"** (`data/claims/C5.yaml`), epoch −10499.
  - Keys: `star.regulus.rise.azimuth`, `star.regulus.rise.lst`, `sun.equinox.rise.lst`, `sun.equinox.rise.azimuth`. Sources: `hancock-bauval-1996` for, `krupp-1997` against, `vondrak-2011` for context.
  - *Comparison 1:* Regulus rose about 10° south of due east. Its declination had precessed to about −8.9°. That is twice the 5° band, so the "Leo rose due east" reading fails.
  - *Comparison 2:* Regulus rose about 12.6° of sidereal time (about 50 minutes) before the equinox sun, inside the ±30° band. So the "Leo rose just before the sun" reading holds.
  - This is the honest receipt: the gaze is right, the azimuth is off.
- **The due-east gaze is not a date.** The equinox sun rises close to due east at every epoch, so the gaze alone dates nothing. Only the Leo part carries a date. The chapter should say so.
- **D4 "Sphinx axis and the temples"**, from the survey figures Nell & Ruggles report (`nell-ruggles-2014`).
  - The Sphinx Temple's east wall is −0°01′12″ off the meridian, and its north wall +0°22′ off due east. Both hold.
  - This is the physical basis of the reply's equinox reading: the temple, not the lion, carries an equinox axis that can be measured.
- **C6 "Solstice akhet"**, Lehner's reading of the plateau. From the Sphinx, the summer solstice sun sets in the gap between Khufu's and Khafre's pyramids, residual +2.6° inside ±8°. It holds. This is the reply's own alignment, and Seked already draws it.

**What would have to be added:**

1. **The Sphinx's own east–west axis.** None is in the database; C5 and D4 both record the gap as a free choice. It could be scaled from ARCE drawing d-gen-008 against its 10 m survey grid (`arce-sphinx-mapping-oc`), with `method: "scaled from plate"` and a sigma. That would replace "due east" with a measured azimuth and an error bar.
2. **Head and body proportions.**
   - New keys: `sphinx.head.height`, `sphinx.face.width`, `sphinx.head.length` (chin to the back of the nemes), and the body's length and height at the withers.
   - Source: the ARCE Sphinx Project elevations and sections on Open Context (6,048 records, CC BY 4.0), scaled from plate, or Lehner 1991's tables transcribed (`arce-sphinx-1991`). The existing `sphinx.length` (73.0 m, with Lehner's 72.55 m in the note) and `sphinx.height` (20.2 m) are unverified.
   - Possible new claim: head-to-body ratio against a lion and against other royal sphinxes. The standard of comparison is a free choice and must be stated.
   - The same drawings would give the base of Member III. That would let the picture show the head inside the hard bed (reply item 2).
3. **When the vernal sun stood in Leo.**
   - *Needed:* the IAU constellation boundaries (Delporte 1930, in the machine-readable version by Davenhall & Leggett 1989, CDS VI/49), imported into `data/stars/` and never typed. A routine would then:
     - find the vernal point of date from `vondrak.ts` (`ltpecl`, `ltpequ`);
     - precess that point back to the boundaries' B1875 frame;
     - report the epochs at which it crosses Virgo→Leo and Leo→Cancer.
   - *My constant-rate estimate*, to be replaced by that computation (rate about 50.2″ a year; Leo meets the ecliptic at about J2000 λ 138° to 174°, from the dates the sun now enters Leo and Virgo, about 10 August and 16 September):
     - the vernal equinox sun crossed from Virgo into Leo around **10,400 BCE**, give or take a century or two;
     - it left Leo for Cancer around **7,900 BCE**;
     - it passed Regulus (J2000 λ ≈ 149.8°) around **8,700 BCE**, the epoch at which Regulus itself rose due east, but then in the sun's glare.
   - *What follows:* by IAU boundaries, 10,500 BCE puts the equinox sun right at the Leo–Virgo border, at the very start of a Leo "age". By Hancock's equal 30° signs, the age is 10,970–8,810 BC. The date depends on which boundaries you use, and the receipt should show both.
   - *Visibility:* a C5-style sweep over epoch, plus a check of whether Regulus (magnitude 1.35, needing the sun about 10–12° below the horizon) could be seen rising before the equinox sun.

## 4. The archaeologists' reply

1. **The Sphinx belongs to Khafre's complex.**
   - *The evidence:*
     - The Sphinx is a knoll left in a quarry, and the quarry's southern edge respects Khafre's causeway.
     - The Sphinx Temple's walls are built from blocks cut from the Sphinx ditch. Lehner matches the beds layer for layer (Lehner diss., pp. 8–9, as cited by Lacovara).
     - The Sphinx Temple copies the plan of Khafre's valley temple beside it and was left unfinished, abandoned at the end of Khafre's reign.
     - No pottery or other artefact anywhere near the Sphinx is older than the Fourth Dynasty.
     - The paws and tail had to be completed in masonry, which suggests a remnant of rock being made into a figure rather than a figure designed from scratch.
     - Rock-cut sculpture first appears under Khafre, for example the tomb of Meresankh III and the pseudo-mastabas in Khafre's quarry.
   - *Citations:*
     - Lehner 1992, "Reconstructing the Sphinx", *Cambridge Archaeological Journal* 2(1): 3–26. **[checked: abstract and pages through Cambridge and the Harvard Giza listing; the PDF itself would not load]**
     - Lehner 2002, "Unfinished Business: The Great Sphinx. Why It Is Most Probable that Khafre Created the Sphinx", *AERAGRAM* 5(2): 10–15. **[checked through Lacovara's citation]**
     - Lehner & Hawass 2017, pp. 229–231 (`lehner-hawass-2017`). **[checked through Lacovara]**
     - Hawass 1993, "The Great Sphinx at Giza: Date and Function", *Sesto Congresso Internazionale di Egittologia, Atti* II, Turin, pp. 177–195. **[checked: bibliographic record, Harvard Giza]**
     - Hawass & Lehner 1994, "The Sphinx: Who Built It, and Why?", *Archaeology* 47(5): 30–41. **[checked: bibliographic record through JSTOR's table of contents]**
     - Lehner 1991 diss. (`arce-sphinx-1991`).
   - *Peer review:* the CAJ article and the Turin proceedings are refereed. The AERAGRAM, *Archaeology* and the 2017 book are not.
   - *The newest statement:* Lacovara's review of Reader, *AJA* 131(1), January 2027, online 6 October 2026. It sets out every point above and calls the older-Sphinx claims "fraudulent". **[checked: full text read]**
   - *For it to be wrong:* the ditch-to-temple block match and the causeway's relation to the quarry would both have to be misread.

2. **The head is small and well kept because of the rock it is in.**
   - *The geology:*
     - The body is cut from Member II: seven beds that alternate hard and soft, and weather badly.
     - The neck and head are in Member III, "softer at the neck and harder at the head".
     - Member III is good building stone, which explains why the face is so much better preserved than the body.
   - *Citations:*
     - The AERA page "Geology of the Sphinx" (© 2025), using Gauri's member labels. **[checked, fetched]**
     - Gauri, Sinai & Bandyopadhyay 1995, "Geologic weathering and its implications on the age of the Sphinx", *Geoarchaeology* 10(2): 119–133, peer reviewed. It argues that salt weathering of these alternating strata, in an arid climate, can produce the rounded profile. **[unchecked: the abstract as quoted in search results; I did not reach the paper]**
   - *Supporting lab work:* Boury, Weady & Ristroph 2023, "Sculpting the Sphinx", *Physical Review Fluids* 8: 110503, peer reviewed, a Gallery of Fluid Motion paper. In a water tunnel, clay mounds with a harder inclusion eroded into a "head", undercut neck, paws and arched back. This supports the 1981 yardang hypothesis: the rough form was natural, and its harder knob became the head. **[checked: bibliographic data through the author's page and press coverage; paper not read]**
   - *The other half of the explanation, from Lehner:* the body's length was set by the rock that was there, and its outline has since been lost and then rebuilt in masonry casing, so today's body is not the original. **[unchecked: no page found]**
   - *For it to be wrong:* the head would have to sit below the top of Member III, or a re-cut would have to be needed to explain the head's size even allowing for the rock. Seked's proportion receipt (§3, item 2) would test this.

3. **The face is within the range of Fourth Dynasty royal portraits.**
   - *The argument:*
     - Old Kingdom royal images are idealised, and identity came from inscriptions, not likeness (W. S. Smith, as cited by catchpenny.org).
     - The Sphinx's face is asymmetric and carved by a team in fissured rock.
     - The scholars who doubt it is Khafre propose only other Fourth Dynasty kings, for example Djedefre or Khufu.
     - The nemes, the uraeus and the later-added beard are dynastic regalia.
   - *Citations:* Lacovara 2026 **[checked]**; catchpenny.org **[checked, a secondary web page]**.
   - *Note:* this concedes that Domingo may be right that the face is not Khafre's, while denying that this makes it older.

4. **The zodiacal Leo is late and foreign.**
   - *Mesopotamia:* the lion constellation is Mesopotamian, UR.GU.LA in MUL.APIN, compiled around 1000 BCE. Rogers traces the Mesopotamian figures through stages between about 3200 and 500 BC.
     - Rogers 1998, *Journal of the British Astronomical Association* 108: 9–28, peer reviewed. **[checked: ADS abstract]**
   - *The twelve-sign zodiac:* invented in Babylonia around the end of the fifth century BCE.
     - Britton 2010, *Archive for History of Exact Sciences* 64(6): 617–663, peer reviewed. **[unchecked: the date is Steele's 2020 summary of it]**
   - *Egypt:* the zodiac does not appear in Egypt until the Ptolemaic period.
     - Christian Leitz on the Esna ceiling, 2023 press coverage. **[unchecked: secondary]**
     - The Dendera round zodiac dates to about 50 BCE from its planetary configuration: Aubourg 1995, BIFAO 95: 1–10, peer reviewed. **[checked: IFAO listing through search]**
     - The earliest pictorial zodiac from Egypt is a papyrus, P.Kramer 17, of 56/55 BC: Winkler & Zellmann-Rohrer 2023. **[unchecked: secondary]**
   - *The proponents' partial counter:* Egypt had a native "Lion" in the northern group of constellations on New Kingdom ceilings, listed in Neugebauer & Parker, *Egyptian Astronomical Texts* III, 1969, though matching it to Leo is unproven. Pliny links Leo to the Nile flood. **[unchecked]**
   - *Strength:* this is the strongest astronomical reply. It needs no computation, only the question of who drew Leo as a lion, and when.

5. **The Sphinx's solar alignments are Khafre's.**
   - *The alignments:*
     - At the equinoxes, the Sphinx Temple's east–west axis, marked by its two sanctuaries, runs over the Sphinx's shoulder to the sun setting at the south foot of Khafre's pyramid. **[unchecked: Hawass & Lehner 1994 as summarised in a search result]**
     - Ricke read the two sanctuaries as the rising and the setting sun. Ricke 1970 is not checked; I found no record.
     - Lehner's akhet: from the Sphinx Temple, the summer solstice sun sets midway between Khufu's and Khafre's pyramids, drawing the horizon hieroglyph. Lehner 1985, "A contextual approach to the Giza Pyramids", *Archiv für Orientforschung*. **[unchecked: the volume is given as 31 or 32 depending on the source, pp. 136–158, as listed in an arXiv bibliography]**
     - Egypt's antiquities ministry photographed the equinox sunset over the Sphinx's shoulder in March 2020 (Live Science and *Archaeology* news).
   - *Receipts:* Seked's D4 and C6 both hold.
   - *What it argues:* the lion's eastward gaze belongs to a solar programme built into Khafre's complex. The later name Horemakhet, "Horus in the horizon", fits that reading. No date in Leo is needed.

6. **The only direct date of the complex is the third millennium BCE.**
   - *Citation:* Liritzis & Vafiadou 2015, "Surface luminescence dating of some Egyptian monuments", *Journal of Cultural Heritage* 16: 134–150, peer reviewed. **[checked: pp. 134 and 147–148 read]**
   - *Results:*
     - Valley Temple: 3000 ± 420 BC, plus a New Kingdom date of 1050 ± 540 BC.
     - Sphinx Temple: ages that "concur with the swayed opinion of a 3rd millennium BC age", with a hint of the early third millennium.
     - The authors say some dates come out a few hundred years older than convention, not thousands.
   - *Strength:* these date when the temple blocks were laid, not when the Sphinx was carved. But the blocks come from the Sphinx ditch (item 1).

## 5. Where it stands

The observation the chapter rests on is real and not in dispute: the head is small for the body and better preserved than it. A re-cut head is still argued for by serious people, most recently Reader (2026), who thinks the original was an Early Dynastic lion. But even Reader's version takes the monument back only a few centuries, not to 10,500 BCE. The sky part fits partly, as Seked's C5 already shows: Leo did rise just before the equinox sun around then, but well south of the Sphinx's gaze. It also depends on a lion constellation for which there is no evidence in Egypt before the Ptolemaic period. Egyptology's case for Khafre, set out in full in Lacovara's October 2026 review, rests on the quarry, the blocks and the temples rather than on the face, and none of the post-1990s work has weakened it.
