## 0. Cold open

**Narration** (70 words, original 113)

Today's Sphinx wears a pharaoh's head, and the textbooks say Khafre's workmen carved it about 2500 BCE. Graham Hancock, Robert Bauval and the geologist Robert Schoch say it began as a lion, in a green Sahara, about 10,500 BCE.

So how old is it? The walls of the ditch are where Schoch started. This piece gives their case as strongly as it can be made, and then the archaeologists' answer.

**Spoken reply** (21 words)

Archaeologists answer that the Sphinx was cut from Khafre's quarry about 2500 BCE. Each chapter sets their reasons beside the claim.

- "Robert Schoch, a geologist, says the rock agrees with them." Folded into the naming of the three, so the two sentences carry the conflict: textbook date against claimed date, today's pharaoh against the green-Sahara lion.

## 1. Older than it should be

**Narration** (139 words, original 219)

The Sphinx was not built. It was carved from bedrock, so the walls of its ditch are as old as the statue.

The geologist Robert Schoch read those walls as rain-cut: soft beds cut back into coves, fissures widest at the top. Wind and sand, he says, cut sharp level grooves. Even Colin Reader, who rejects his date, agrees water cut them. Archaeologists answer that the ditch was Khafre's quarry.

Then he looked under the floor. A 1992 seismic survey, by his account, found the rock weathered about two metres deep in front and a little over one behind. Deeper means longer exposed, so the front was cut first. On his most cautious reckoning the core is at least seven thousand years old; he prefers about 10,000 BCE. Other geologists say the rock's own layers could explain the difference.

**Spoken reply** (34 words)

Archaeologists answer that the ditch was quarried for Khafre's temples, and geologists that old cracks and the rock's own layers can explain the wear. No one has drilled the floor to test either reading.

**Cut, and why**

- "It was carved, by cutting a ditch around a knoll of bedrock." Shortened to "carved from bedrock"; the ditch is in the next clause.
- "The profile rolls. Harder beds stand out", "where water off the plateau would run in", and "like those on the Old Kingdom tombs nearby". Detail the stage shows (the beats draw the bedding, the run-off arrows and the tomb photograph); the ear keeps coves and top-heavy fissures.
- "In a seismic survey published in 1992, he and a colleague sent sound through the rock." Reduced to "A 1992 seismic survey". The critic found the sentence had no picture; the beats now have to supply the geophones.
- "In the early 1990s." Dropped, not rewritten. Open question 3 in `docs/script.md` says the date Schoch first looked is unchecked; the survey year is the checked one.
- "If the body is that old, look again at the head." Cut as the critic asks: chapter 2 opens "Look at the head."
- "Deeper weathering means longer exposure, he argues" shortened to "Deeper means longer exposed".

## 2. A lion, not a king

**Narration** (153 words, original 219)

Look at the head. It wears a Fourth Dynasty king's headcloth, it is better kept than the body, and it is too small for it. Nobody disputes that.

Hancock, Bauval and Schoch say the figure was a lion first. Rain wore its head away, and a king of the pyramid age had a royal face cut into what was left. You can only carve a smaller form inside a larger one, so the new head came out small. Egyptologists say the head is small because the rock is harder there.

The equinox sun rises almost due east in every age, so the gaze dates nothing. But the stars behind it drift. About 10,500 BCE, Hancock and Bauval calculate, the constellation Leo rose in the east just before the equinox sun. Egyptologists add that Leo is a Mesopotamian figure, late in Egypt.

If the lion is that old, they argue, so are its temples.

**Spoken reply** (32 words)

Egyptologists answer that the head is small because it was cut from a harder band of rock, and that the Sphinx fits Khafre's quarry, his temples and their alignment to the sun.

**Cut, and why**

- "Read the two facts as one story." The next sentence is the story.
- "It stood through the long rains" and "his sculptors cut a royal face": merged into one sentence.
- The Reader sentence ("argued in 2026 that it began as a lion", 12 words). Reader still appears in chapter 1 and in this chapter's reply panel through Lacovara. It can come back if the owner wants the supporting voice in this chapter too.
- "The lion on the ground watched its own image come up." The critic's example of a poetic line that drags a point already understood.
- "Close to due east" became "almost due east", and "In the east" stays: the receipt's 10 degree miss (C5) is drawn by the beat and stated in the receipt, as open question 15 asks.

## 3. Temples of the same stone

**Narration** (135 words, original 218)

At the Sphinx's paws stand two temples. The Valley Temple is lined with red granite over a limestone core, in blocks that Flinders Petrie found ranging to over a hundred tons each. Those blocks were cut from the Sphinx's own ditch; all sides agree.

John Anthony West and Schoch went further. In a 1993 film they said the limestone behind the granite is rounded and fissured like the ditch walls, and the granite was cut to fit it. Nobody weathers a wall and then cases it, they say: the granite is a refit.

Mark Lehner answers that the exposed cores weathered only after being cleared in the early 1900s.

Khafre's statues, found inside, show he used the temple. They leave its core's age open.

And Egypt's own records put long ages before its first king.

**Spoken reply** (26 words)

Archaeologists answer that core and casing went up together, as at Menkaure's unfinished temple, and that the Sphinx Temple never received its outer granite at all.

**Cut, and why**

- "On camera in 1993" became "in a 1993 film". The sentence naming Schoch's own claim about the core blocks of both temples carrying rain weathering was cut as a repeat of West's and Schoch's claim.
- Hancock's "on no good grounds whatsoever" (14 words, with the lead-in). It voices the claim but adds no fact the receipts need. It is the first thing to restore if the owner wants a third proponent in this chapter.

## 4. The First Time

**Narration** (156 words, original 219)

A papyrus in Turin, copied about 1250 BCE, lists kings back to Menes, and before him gods, spirits and Followers of Horus. On its most probable reading, the spirits and Followers get 13,420 years and, in the next line, 23,200. The papyrus is some 1,850 years younger than Menes, and partly restored.

The Egyptians called that beginning sep tepi, the First Time. Hancock and Bauval read it as memory, not myth.

A stela by the Great Pyramid has Khufu finding a house of Isis "beside the house of the Sphinx". So, they argue, the Sphinx predates Khafre. Its translator, James Breasted, called the evidence for a late date "entirely conclusive."

In King Unas's tomb, about 2400 BCE, the oldest Pyramid Texts say "Unas appears as a star" and "Orion is encircled by the Duat," with Sothis, our Sirius, and Unas.

None of these texts gives a date. Hancock and Bauval look for one in the sky.

**Spoken reply** (34 words)

Egyptologists answer that every one of these texts was written long after the time it describes, that none carries a date, and that radiocarbon dates Egypt's first king to about 3100 BCE without them.

**Cut, and why**

- "Egypt did not think it began with its first king." The papyrus sentence says it.
- "Manetho, a thousand years later, tells the same story." Manetho and Eusebius stay in the reply panel and the receipts; the narration needs the one papyrus. This is the first thing to restore if the owner wants Manetho heard.
- "A golden age, they write, that immediately followed creation", and the paragraph on the plateau as they picture it. The picture shows it (the beats' First Time wide shot).
- "Found beside", "on the walls of ... burial chambers at Saqqara" and "carved": place and setting detail. Unas's tomb and the date stay.
- "The most probable reading", the 13,420 and 23,200 and the "next line" wording are kept as written, because the receipts depend on them and open question 6 asks that "next line" avoid committing to whether the second figure includes the first.

## 5. The sky as a clock

**Narration** (167 words, original 219)

In 1989 Robert Bauval argued that Giza's three pyramids copy Orion's belt: two nearly equal, the third smaller and off the line, like Mintaka. A 2026 study ranks the match in the top quarter of one per cent of almost 700,000 star triplets, but calls it "real but narrow."

Bauval and Hancock read the plan as a clock. The Earth's wobble raises and lowers the belt over 26,000 years, and about 10,500 BCE it hits the bottom of its swing: the First Time, they say. But Seked's engine puts the belt thirteen degrees off the pyramids' line then, and the swing is too flat to name a century.

Inside the Great Pyramid, a shaft from the King's Chamber climbs south at 45 degrees. About 2450 BCE Alnitak, the belt's easternmost star, crossed the meridian within a fifth of a degree of that line. So the stone holds two dates, they say: its building and its plan. The shafts, say astronomers, date only the building, in Khufu's century.

**Spoken reply** (34 words)

Astronomers answer that the shape is real but narrow, that in 10,450 BCE the belt lies thirteen degrees off the pyramids' line, and that the shafts point to Khufu's century, not the Ice Age.

**Cut, and why**

- "To the Egyptians Orion was Sah, Osiris among the stars." The label stays on the beat.
- "Every closer match is a group nobody ever named; Orion's belt is the first that someone did." The strongest line the claim has. Cut only for length; it is the first thing to restore in this chapter.
- "Drag the sky back." An instruction to the interactive figure; the beat carries it.
- "Raises and lowers the belt on the southern meridian" shortened to "raises and lowers the belt".
- "They find numbers in it as well." The bridge into chapter 6.
- Kept on purpose: 1989, "almost 700,000", 26,000 years, 10,500 BCE, 45 degrees, 2450 BCE, "a fifth of a degree", and the 13 degree gap, since the receipt (C4, C2) is built on them. "Seked's engine" is the receipt's own computation, named as in the original reply.

## 6. Numbers in the stone

**Narration** (166 words, original 245)

Measure the Great Pyramid. Walk round its base and divide by its original height: two times pi, within three hundredths of one per cent.

Its slant height over half its base is the golden ratio, within a twentieth of one per cent.

Scale it up 43,200 times and its height is the Earth's polar radius, its perimeter the equator, each within one per cent. Hancock notes that 43,200 is 600 times 72, and 72 years is about how long the stars take to drift one degree.

The sides run within four minutes of arc of true north, a quarter of a metre over 230 metres.

For Hancock, that knowledge was inherited from a civilisation older than Egypt.

Egyptian builders gave slopes as a seked, and five and a half palms of run to each cubit of rise gives both numbers. The survey cannot tell the slopes apart, and 43,200 is a factor chosen to fit. Their papyri teach the seked, but date centuries after the pyramids.

**Spoken reply** (32 words)

Mathematicians answer that one builders' rule, a slope of five and a half palms in seven, gives both pi and the golden ratio, and that 43,200 is a number chosen to fit.

**Cut, and why**

- "Writers since Charles Piazzi Smyth in the 1860s have read these numbers as a design brief" and the circle-golden-section-Earth-sky summary. A history lesson the receipts do not use. Smyth stays in `sources.json` and can come back in the reply panel.
- "With no compass." The quarter-metre figure is kept.
- "Draw a line from the apex down the middle of one face" became "slant height".
- "Each number alone has an ordinary explanation" and "the proponents ask whether the explanations hold when the numbers come together." The seked answer now follows the numbers directly, which makes the same point.
- "Knowledge that old needs people to have held it." The bridge into the timeline.
- Spence's survey (the north alignment improving to Khufu and then slipping) stays in the reply panel; no spoken line was spared for it.

## 7. Before the flood

**Narration** (150 words, original 210)

About 12,900 years ago the warming stopped and the northern world fell back into cold for over a thousand years: the Younger Dryas. Hancock cites about a hundred peer-reviewed studies blaming a comet, with glass melted above 2,200 degrees at Abu Hureyra. Thirteen specialists called that refuted in 2023.

Göbekli Tepe, in south-east Turkey, is a set of rings of T-shaped pillars built about 9500 BCE, seven thousand years before the Great Pyramid. Its builders, the excavators say, were hunters and foragers with no farms, pottery or metal. Klaus Schmidt thought they filled it in on purpose; later excavators say slope slides did.

Schoch points to Göbekli Tepe when asked where the older civilisation was. If non-farmers could raise this, a plan at Giza in the eleventh millennium BCE is not absurd, and for this piece that makes the rest worth taking seriously. Nothing found there links it to Egypt.

**Spoken reply** (33 words)

The excavators answer that Göbekli Tepe was a lived-in place with houses and a possible cistern, filled by slope slides rather than by hand, and that nothing found there links it to Egypt.

**Cut, and why**

- "As the last Ice Age was ending" and "Syria", "an early village": setting a newcomer does not need.
- "Then, as the cold lifts, a monument appears on a hilltop": the hilltop and the staging.
- "Schmidt's rebuttal in full": the 2025 and 2026 retractions, the 2020 Clare summary's details (round houses, the rock-cut pit) and the "comprehensive refutation" quotation all stay in the reply panel; the narration keeps the count (thirteen) and the year (2023).
- The "about 1,100 kilometres" figure was only ever in the reply and is not in the narration (open question 9 calls it arithmetic on approximate coordinates).
- "Some of that evidence has never been gathered." The bridge into chapter 8.

## 8. Underneath

**Narration** (158 words, original 215)

Now the claims go underground.

In March 2025 a team including the radar engineer Filippo Biondi said satellites had found, under Khafre's pyramid, eight wells falling 648 metres, four and a half pyramid heights, to two chambers about 80 metres on a side. The rooms anyone has entered lie three metres below the base. Radar specialists say vibrations from that depth cannot be detected from orbit, and the method's only peer-reviewed paper was retracted in 2026.

At Hawara, Herodotus saw a Labyrinth of three thousand rooms, half underground. Petrie found only a bed of stone chips, larger than the Great Pyramid's base. The expedition behind a 2008 survey reported walls eight to twelve metres down; archaeologists say it was Amenemhat III's temple, and later surveys find only shallow remains.

Under the Sphinx's paws, Schoch's seismic survey found a hollow no one has opened, where Edgar Cayce placed a Hall of Records. Nobody has dug for any of it.

**Spoken reply** (31 words)

Archaeologists answer that the radar method's only peer-reviewed paper was retracted and its data never released, and that the Labyrinth was Amenemhat III's temple, where published surveys find only shallow remains.

**Cut, and why**

- "Radar cannot see far into rock, and Biondi agrees. Their method reads instead the faint vibrations of the ground, the way an ultrasound reads a body." The method explanation. The beat's inset still carries it; the radar specialists' sentence now stands where it was.
- "Two rows" and "wound with spiral paths". The receipt's list of free choices (diameter, spacing, pitch) is unaffected.

## 9. What would settle it

**Narration** (147 words, original 219)

Much of this case can be tested. The one measured support for the early date is Schoch's seismic finding that the rock is weathered more deeply in front of the Sphinx than behind it. No one has drilled to test it. Two cores, at the paws and behind the rump, would show whether it is weathering or a change in the rock.

In 2008 Egypt's antiquities authority and Cairo University drilled four boreholes twenty metres deep around the Sphinx; we have found no published record of its weathering.

Underneath, the paw cavity could be opened, the radar data released, and Hawara dug.

An answer looks like this: muon detectors found a hidden corridor in the Great Pyramid, with error bars, and a camera confirmed it.

Where the case has been measured, the measurements have mostly gone against it. The ditch floor has not been tested, and that is where to look.

**Spoken reply** (35 words)

Archaeologists answer that no physical date from Giza, the first of them paid for by Cayce's own foundation, comes near 10,500 BCE, and that holes drilled around the Sphinx in 1978 found only cracks.

**Cut, and why**

- "Under the Sphinx, the test that matters most has not been made" and "Look beneath the floor of the ditch." Merged into the opening and the second sentence; the "no one has drilled" line makes the point.
- "It can be done." The boreholes sentence now follows without it.
- "Hancock, Bauval and Schoch read the stone, the sky and the texts as one story." The recap, 15 words. The beat of the nine key frames running across the stage can carry it unspoken.
- "Until it is" and the last-sentence echo. The close keeps its verdict ("mostly gone against it", open question 14) and its pointer to the ditch floor.
- The radiocarbon answer (more than 450 samples, 2900 to 2400 BCE) is not in the narration, because this chapter's claim is a test, not a date. It sits in the reply panel and the spoken reply carries the Cayce Foundation point.
